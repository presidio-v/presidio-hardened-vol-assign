## Reviewer 1

The mapping between disaster-induced shocks and economic turbulence remains conceptual analogy without empirical evidence from real-world market data. Quantitative audit results cannot be directly interpreted as real-world performance of commercial AI systems under economic turbulence. The manuscript should tone down cross‑context quantitative extrapolation and retain only mechanistic analogy.

Scalability boundaries of the repaired MIP formulation are insufficiently discussed. Although wall-clock runtime under different instance sizes is reported, the manuscript does not systematically elaborate performance degradation of exact solvers with growing problem scale and additional real-world constraints. Clear boundaries on when evolutionary meta‑heuristics become truly necessary should be supplemented.

Engagement with existing literature on algorithmic auditing and high-risk-AI regulation is weak. The manuscript does not benchmark against established auditing frameworks, AI production-readiness assessment or EU AI-Act evaluation paradigms, and fails to clarify novel checkpoints and incremental methodological contributions compared with prior work.

Practical implications remain generic and lack actionable recommendations for humanitarian practitioners and AI procurement stakeholders.

## Reviewer 2

The manuscript audits the authors' own published fuzzy multi-objective relief-allocation system (ref. [10]) using a five-step protocol: structural check, exact reference, seed floor, input turbulence, and repair with a deployment envelope. The main findings are as follows. The published model is separable and can be solved exactly by a greedy rule. The evolutionary fronts are dominated by a single exact point. Runs with different seeds commit to very different allocations. Input fragility, once measured on the exact decider, is smooth and concentrated in a few inputs. A capacity-aware repair can still be solved exactly at the target sizes. The paper is candid, carefully written, and supported by an open artefact, and the reported numbers are internally consistent where I could check them. Still, I have concerns about novelty, scope, the fairness of the baselines, and parts of the evidence. They are set out below.

1. The central result of RQ1 is that the authors' own previously published model [10] has objectives that are averages of per-person scores with no coupling constraint, so a weighted sum separates by person (Section 4.1, Eq. 1). This is an elementary property. Once it is noticed, the dominance results in Tables 2 and 3 follow almost by necessity. As a result, much of the paper reads as a correction of [10] rather than a new research contribution. The protocol is presented as "domain-agnostic" and as "the deliverable" (lines 46–47, 418), but it is applied to only one system, which the authors built themselves, and step S1 ends the necessity question at once. The authors should state clearly what is new beyond a correction of [10]. I think, they should also show the protocol on at least one more case where S1 does not settle the matter, ideally a system they did not design (the repaired model alone is not enough, since it is also their own construction).

2. The title, abstract and keywords present the paper as a study of "digital AI systems" under "economic turbulence" in supply chains. However, the case is post-disaster relief, the instances are synthetic and produced by the system's own generator (line 401), and the economic link rests only on the analogy in Table 1. The authors say this mapping makes "no claim that numbers transfer" (lines 139–140, 383). The text also calls the system "deployed" (lines 49, 119) without giving any evidence of real use. The authors should either tone down the title, abstract and conclusions to match what is actually tested, or add a case with real economic or supply-chain data. They should also remove or justify the word "deployed," and they should state more directly why the work fits the scope.

3. Many elements the results depend on are only defined in [10]: the fuzzy rule bases and membership functions, the instance generator, the chromosome encoding and variation operators of the evolutionary solvers, how the selection of exactly n_dir directed people is handled, and the "crisp priority rule" used as a reference in Section 5.3 and Figure 4. Symbols in Eq. (2), such as FIS3, COR_i, RDR_i and TD_j,i, are never defined in this paper. A reader cannot judge the results without going to another paper and the code. The authors should add a compact model section that formally defines all objectives, inputs and symbols. It should summarise the fuzzy systems (number of rules, inputs, output ranges), describe the encoding and operators, and include a table of all algorithm parameters (population, crossover and mutation settings, termination) for every solver used.

4. The main comparisons use a budget of 150 generations with population 100, and in the deployment envelope NSGA-II "has no capacity handling" (line 323) yet is compared against a MIP built for the repaired model. A generic stochastic search losing to an exact method on a separable problem, or on a model it was not designed for, is expected and says little about evolutionary search in general. It is also not clear whether the wall-clock times in Table 6 include the fuzzy pre-computation (0.05–0.22 s with the compiled evaluator, 4–16 s with scikit-fuzzy, lines 216–217), whether the MIP's CAIL-per-load-level tables are counted, or whether NSGA-II used the compiled evaluator. The authors should justify the encoding and budget. They should add at least one stronger heuristic reference, for example an MOEA seeded with greedy solutions, an MOEA with a capacity-repair operator, or a simple local search on the repaired model. They should also report end-to-end pipeline times for all methods under the same accounting, and soften statements such as "only a capacity-aware heuristic could claim that regime" (line 340) unless sucha heuristic is actually tested.

5. The high churn (0.83 and 0.94) is measured between committed decisions taken from fronts that are far from the optimum (Table 2). It may therefore reflect non-convergence of the solver rather than a basic property of seed-dependent systems. It may also reflect a flat objective landscape in which many quite different allocations have almost the same cost. These explanations have different consequences, and the paper does not separate them. The claim that the exact decider "by construction" has a floor of zero (line 255) also holds only under a fixed tie-breaking rule. Appendix C shows that the hard-capacity MIP already returns two different optimal allocations on two platforms. The authors should quantify how many substantially different allocations lie within a small tolerance ε of the exact optimum, and report the churn and Jaccard index among these near-optimal exact solutions. This would show how much of the churn is due to the solver and how much to the problem. I think, they should discuss the tie-breaking issue in the main text, not only in an appendix, and they should relate their notion of identifiability to existing work on solution multiplicity, such as the literature on predictive multiplicity and the "Rashomon effect" in machine-learning auditing, which deals with a closely related question.

6. Inputs are perturbed one field at a time (the authors note this, line 403). The levels are fractions of each field's range, and the claim that levels 0.05–0.1 are "most relevant in practice" (line 298) is not supported by any data or reference. Level 0 is an identity check with zero regret by construction, and it is not clear whether it is included in the one-sided Spearman trend tests. If it is, the very small p-values (< 10⁻⁶) are partly guaranteed by design. The analysis of the evolutionary system uses only 12 and 8 realisations and 8 and 6 seeds (line 293), and Table 2 mixes 20, 10 and 5 seeds across rows. The authors should state exactly which levels enter the trend tests and repeat them without level 0 if needed. They should justify the turbulence levels with empirical evidence on data-quality errors in relief or supply-chain settings, and add at least a small set of joint (multi-field) perturbations. They should also report confidence intervals for all rows of Table 2, using the same number of seeds where possible.

7. The abstract states that the repaired model is solved "to proven optimality with hard capacity on a 3000-person instance in five minutes." According to the caption of Table 6, however, all 300 s rows are single instances (seed 0). In the 3000 × 50 row at 60 s, the soft MIP quality (154.9) is averaged over the two instances where an incumbent was found, while NSGA-II (145.4) is averaged over three, so the statement that the evolutionary system "beats" the MIP compares different instance sets. The deterministic fallback is reported next to the other methods on quality alone, although it overloads centres. The repair formula in Eq. (2), linear in load and clipped at 100, is introduced without justification or sensitivity analysis, even though the finding on CAIL's limited "dynamic range" (17–20 points) depends on the rule base taken as published. The authors should run the 300 s experiments on at least as many instances as the 60 s experiments and report results per instance. They should compare methods on the same instance sets, add overload columns for NSGA-II and the fallback in Table 6, test at least one alternative form of the repair, and adjust the abstract to reflect the actual sample size.

## Reviewer 3

Summary
The manuscript presents a five-step audit of the authors' previously published fuzzy multi-objective system for assigning people to relief centers. It examines whether evolutionary optimisation is necessary, whether different random seeds produce consistent allocations, and how degraded input data affect decision quality. The authors report that a simple exact method outperforms the evolutionary solvers for the original model, that allocations vary substantially between runs, and that a repaired model incorporating center loads remains tractable at the intended problem sizes. The main contribution is a practical audit protocol combining structural analysis, exact baselines, stability testing and input-sensitivity analysis.
Overall, this is a promising and clearly structured manuscript. Its strongest contribution is the demonstration that structural analysis and appropriate baselines can reveal weaknesses hidden by comparisons between evolutionary algorithms alone. The authors' critical reassessment of their own work is valuable. However, several central claims require qualification, and important methodological details need clarification before publication.

Critical notes and points for improvement:

**Clarify what the study establishes about the necessity of AI.**
The experiments demonstrate that evolutionary search is unnecessary for the particular published allocation model. They do not establish that AI generally is unnecessary: the exact method still uses the fuzzy inference scores. The title, abstract and conclusions should distinguish the necessity of the stochastic optimiser from the usefulness of the wider fuzzy decision-support system. The novelty of the protocol should also be positioned more precisely against existing optimisation benchmarking, sensitivity analysis and algorithmic auditing practices.

**Correct the description of the "exact supported front."**
Solving weighted sums exactly at a finite grid of weight vectors does not demonstrate that every supported nondominated point has been recovered. This limitation is additional to the acknowledged omission of unsupported Pareto points. Exact multi-objective methods distinguish solving individual scalarisations from enumerating the relevant solution set. Unless completeness is established, the manuscript should describe this as a sampled supported reference front. A finer-grid sensitivity analysis would help establish whether the reported hypervolume comparisons are stable. This correction would preserve the convincing dominance findings while avoiding an overstated reference benchmark.

**Separate decision stability from uniqueness and accountability.**
The substantial variation between seeds is an important finding. However, variation does not by itself mean that an output is not a decision or cannot be accountable. A documented randomised allocation policy can be legitimate, while a deterministic rule can select arbitrarily among equally good alternatives. Appendix C is particularly relevant because it reports different optimal allocations from the exact solver. The discussion should distinguish solver non-convergence, multiple optimal or near-optimal solutions, and the policy used to select among them. "Decision stability" would communicate the measured property more directly than "identifiability." The proposed deterministic tie-breaking rule should also be demonstrated rather than presented only as a recommendation.

**Provide the essential methodological details within the manuscript.**
Several components are insufficiently specified for readers to assess the experiments without reconstructing the software. These include:
- The crisp priority baseline, including its ranking and assignment rules.
- The exact formula and denominator for allocation churn, including people selected in only one allocation.
- The evolutionary representation, variation operators and principal parameter settings.
- The instance-generation distributions and relevant input dependencies.
- The calculation used to measure turbulence effects "above" the seed baseline.

Systematic bias is listed among the perturbation modes, but the main reported results cover noise, missing values and categorical flips. Bias results should be reported or the stated experimental coverage revised.

**Strengthen the basis for generalisation.**
The seed experiments provide useful evidence about variability on fixed instances, but repeated seeds and perturbations do not replace independent problem instances. The fragility findings should be tested across more generated instances and a wider range of occupancy, capacity and demand conditions. Joint or correlated perturbations would also be informative, although their absence is appropriately acknowledged. Claims that particular perturbation levels are most relevant in practice, or that the resulting fragility is manageable, require operational justification. Without such evidence, conclusions should remain explicitly about synthetic instances and model-based losses.

**Clarify the deployment comparison and treatment of unsuccessful solves.**
Table 6 reports cases where the MIP finds no incumbent. Explain how these failures enter the reported quality averages and Figure 5, and ensure comparisons use matching instances. The single-instance five-minute results should not imply a general performance boundary. Since the inexpensive deterministic fallback sometimes outperforms the time-limited MIP, discuss whether it could provide an initial incumbent or form part of a combined deployment strategy. Continue distinguishing optimisation time from fuzzy-score preparation, and report total decision latency alongside solver-only timings.

**Qualify the operational interpretation of the results.**
Regret is measured against the model's own fuzzy objectives, rather than observed humanitarian outcomes. Lower objective values therefore demonstrate better optimisation of the specified model, not necessarily better real-world decisions. Similarly, making occupancy endogenous does not consistently reduce maximum load or overload in every reported case. The discussion should distinguish correcting the model's structure from demonstrating improved operational safety or balance.

Several smaller remarks would also improve the manuscript:
- Figures 4 and 5 would benefit from larger labels. Figure 5 should identify unsuccessful solves explicitly.
- The conclusions should distinguish: findings established analytically, findings supported by the experiments, and proposed implications for other domains.
