\abstract{%
Digital AI systems are adopted to allocate scarce resources under economic turbulence on two
untested assumptions: that the problem needs the AI, and that the AI's output is a decision
rather than one sample of many. We audit both, and input fragility, on our own published
system: a fuzzy multi-objective tool (Mamdani inference, NSGA-II/NRGA) that directs affected
people to relief centres, a shock we map mechanism by mechanism to economic turbulence. A five-step protocol finds that the published
model is separable: a deterministic rule computes its exact optimum in microseconds, and one
such point dominates 97--100\% of the front points in our published experiments. Two runs
on identical data with different seeds commit to allocations that differ for 83--94\% of
the people directed; seed pinning makes the system reproducible, not identifiable. On the
exact decider, the regret of deciding on degraded data grows with turbulence and concentrates
in the inputs the objectives consume. A minimal repair that
lets the redundancy objective see the load it creates is still solved exactly in seconds at
the system's target sizes, and to proven optimality with hard capacity on a 3000-person
instance in five minutes. The protocol is domain-agnostic and checkable from the open
artefact.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Turbulence breaks two things at once: the balance between demand and capacity, and the
data a decision rests on. An uncertainty shock depresses activity because firms cannot
read their own situation~\cite{bloom2009uncertainty}, and that uncertainty can be measured
and tracked~\cite{baker2016epu}; supply networks that must stay viable through such
periods are asked to decide while their inputs are late, noisy or missing~\cite{ivanov2020viability,ivanov2020covid}.
Digital AI systems are increasingly placed in exactly this position. They ingest
situational data, search a large decision space, and commit to an allocation that people
then carry out~\cite{modgil2022ai,belhadi2024ai,pan2025aiusage}.

Adopting such a system rests on two assumptions that are rarely tested. The first is
\emph{necessity}: that the decision problem is hard enough to need the AI, here a
stochastic multi-objective evolutionary search, rather than an exact method. The second is
\emph{identifiability}: that the system's output is a decision, in the sense that running
it again on the same data yields the same allocation. We use the word for the committed
output of a decision system, not, as in statistics, for model parameters: a system is
identifiable when independent runs on the same inputs commit to the same decision. If
either assumption fails, a system can pass every accuracy check and still be the wrong
tool for a turbulent environment. Its quality is then bought at a cost it did not need to
pay, and its output cannot be held to account.

This paper audits one such system, and the system is our own. The fuzzy multi-objective
allocation tool that directs affected people to relief centres after a disaster grew out of
our earlier models~\cite{rabiei2021vehicle,rabiei2023volunteer} and was published, with
an open reference implementation, as a reproducible four-objective
framework~\cite{rabiei2026resilient}. It combines Mamdani fuzzy inference with NSGA-II and
NRGA, it is laptop-deployable, and its decisions are bit-for-bit reproducible. We expected
an audit of its behaviour under input turbulence to confirm that the fuzzy front-end makes
the system degrade gracefully. It did not, and the reason was not where we looked first.

We report the audit as a reusable protocol of five steps: a structural check of the
optimisation model, an exact reference and dominance test, a seed floor and budget curve
for the stochastic optimiser, an input-turbulence study on a noise-free decider, and a
repair with a deployment envelope. Relief allocation after a disaster is an acute
resource-allocation shock; we use it as the case and engage the economic-turbulence
setting through an explicit mapping of shock mechanisms (Section~\ref{sec:case}), not with
market data. The protocol itself is domain-agnostic, and it is the deliverable for any
organisation that adopts an optimising AI system for turbulent conditions.

Four research questions structure the audit:
\begin{itemize}
\item[RQ1] (\emph{Necessity}) Does the deployed optimisation problem require the
evolutionary search?
\item[RQ2] (\emph{Identifiability}) Is the system's committed decision identifiable, or
only reproducible?
\item[RQ3] (\emph{Input fragility}) How much does a decision lose when it is made on
degraded data, once solver noise is removed?
\item[RQ4] (\emph{Repair and envelope}) Does a minimal semantic repair of the model change
these answers, and where does exact optimisation stop being practical?
\end{itemize}

The answers are uncomfortable for the system and useful for its users. The published
model is separable: a deterministic greedy rule computes the exact optimum of any weighted
objective sum in milliseconds, and one such point dominates 97--100\% of the front points the
evolutionary solvers returned in our own published experiments. The committed
decision of the evolutionary system differs for most of the directed people between two
runs on identical data, a property that seed pinning hides but does not remove. Measured on
the exact decider, the regret of deciding on degraded data grows smoothly and is
concentrated in the inputs the objectives consume. A minimal repair that makes one
objective depend on the load it creates restores the intended semantics, yet the repaired
model is still solved exactly in seconds at the sizes the system targets. The contributions
are the protocol, the four findings, the open artefact that makes each of them checkable,
and a correction of our own prior work that we state in the open.

Section~\ref{sec:background} positions the audit; Section~\ref{sec:case} describes the
system and the turbulence mapping; Section~\ref{sec:protocol} defines the protocol;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports RQ1--RQ4; Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}
and~\ref{sec:conclusion} draw the implications.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Decision systems under deep uncertainty}
A decision aid can absorb only as much environmental variety as it can represent; Ashby's
law of requisite variety~\cite{ashby1956cybernetics} is the systems statement of that
limit, and bounded rationality~\cite{simon1955behavioral} is its behavioural counterpart.
Operational research answered with robustness as a criterion in its own
right~\cite{rosenhead1972robustness}, with warnings against models that are elegant but
detached from the problem they claim to represent~\cite{ackoff1979future}, and, more
recently, with methods that evaluate decisions across many plausible futures rather than
one forecast~\cite{lempert2006robust,walker2003uncertainty,marchau2019deep}. Robust
optimisation turns the same concern into tractable models~\cite{bental2002robust,bertsimas2004price}.
Our audit borrows the stance and applies it to the decision system itself: before asking
how a system responds to turbulence, ask whether its structure matches the problem.

\subsection{Multi-objective search, exact methods and their critique}
Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II~\cite{deb2002nsga2}, NRGA~\cite{aljadaan2008nrga}
and NSGA-III~\cite{deb2014nsga3} return an approximation of the Pareto front and leave the
final commitment to a selection rule~\cite{branke2004knees}. Their behaviour under noisy
fitness and uncertain parameters is well studied~\cite{jin2005uncertain,deb2006robustness,beyer2007robust,ide2016robustness},
and front quality is commonly measured by hypervolume~\cite{zitzler1999spea}. The theory of
multicriteria optimisation is equally clear that weighted-sum scalarisation recovers every
supported Pareto point~\cite{ehrgott2005multicriteria,das1998nbi}, and exact methods for
multi-objective combinatorial problems are mature~\cite{ehrgott2000moco}. A heuristic must
therefore justify itself against an exact baseline~\cite{hooker1995testing}; no algorithm
is better than another across all problems~\cite{wolpert1997nfl}, so the choice should
follow from problem features~\cite{kerschke2019selection}. The metaheuristics community has
criticised the habit of skipping this step~\cite{sorensen2015metaphor,aranha2022metaphor},
and benchmarking guidelines now require seeds, baselines and complete
reporting~\cite{bartzbeielstein2020benchmarking}. Reproducibility of evolutionary
computation, including the dependence of outcomes on random seeds, has its own
literature~\cite{lopezibanez2021reproducibility}.

\subsection{Fuzzy inference in decision support}
Fuzzy sets~\cite{zadeh1965fuzzy} and Mamdani inference~\cite{mamdani1975linguistic} encode
expert judgement as linguistic rules over graded memberships, and fuzzy multi-criteria
methods are widespread in decision support~\cite{kahraman2015fuzzymcdm}. Uncertainty in
the memberships themselves is the domain of type-2 systems~\cite{mendel2017uncertain,castillo2008type2}.
The audited system uses type-1 Mamdani inference, implemented with
scikit-fuzzy~\cite{warner2019skfuzzy}, to turn situational inputs into objective scores.

\subsection{Humanitarian operations}
Disaster operations research has a long review
tradition~\cite{altay2006ormsdisaster,galindo2013review,caunhye2012emergency}, and
post-disaster logistics differs structurally from commercial logistics through demand
surges, deprivation and degraded information~\cite{holguinveras2012unique,kovacs2007humanitarian,sheu2007emergency}.
Practitioners weigh several objectives at once~\cite{gralla2014tradeoffs}, multi-criteria
models are common~\cite{gutjahr2016multicriteria}, and the field has asked for models that
are implementable rather than only elegant~\cite{besiou2020humanitarian}. Closest to the
audited problem, volunteer and resource assignment under uncertainty has been solved
exactly by robust optimisation~\cite{lassiter2015volunteer}. Resilience
framings~\cite{bruneau2003resilience,christopher2004resilient,sheffi2005resilient,hosseini2019review}
connect these operations to the supply-chain literature on
disruption~\cite{queiroz2022epidemic,aghsami2024humanitarian}.

\subsection{Data quality, trust and auditing of AI systems}
Data quality has several dimensions, among them accuracy, completeness and
timeliness~\cite{wang1996dataquality}, and in high-stakes AI upstream data problems
compound into downstream harm~\cite{sambasivan2021cascades}. Deployment conditions differ
from development conditions~\cite{quinonerocandela2008shift,amodei2016concrete}.
Trust in an AI system is warranted only when the system has the properties the trust
assumes~\cite{jacovi2021trust}, and accountability requires decision procedures that can
be verified after the fact~\cite{kroll2017accountable,doshivelez2017interpretable}.
Algorithmic auditing turns these demands into procedures, from internal end-to-end
frameworks~\cite{raji2020audit} to ethics-based~\cite{mokander2021ethics} and external
audits~\cite{metaxa2021auditing}; production-readiness tests do the same for machine-learning
pipelines~\cite{breck2017mltestscore}. Regulation now codifies record-keeping, accuracy and
robustness duties for high-risk systems~\cite{eu2024aiact}, while computational science
treats reproducibility as a precondition of trust~\cite{peng2011reproducible,stodden2016enhancing,gundersen2018reproducibility,pineau2021reproducibility,wilkinson2016fair}.

\subsection{Gap}
Audits of AI systems mostly examine predictive models: their accuracy, fairness,
transparency and documentation. Optimisation-based decision systems raise two further
questions that these frameworks do not ask. Is the search needed at all, given the
structure of the model? And is the committed output a decision, or one sample from a
distribution of decisions? We found no audit of a deployed optimisation-based decision
system that tests necessity and identifiability alongside input fragility; this paper
provides one, and turns it into a protocol.

\section{The Case: Relief Allocation as an Acute Turbulence Shock}\label{sec:case}

\subsection{The audited system}
The system directs $n_{\mathrm{dir}}$ of $m$ affected people to $n$ relief centres. It
reads per-person attributes (age, disability, injury and living status, local
infrastructure-damage level, remaining resource time), per-centre state (occupancy and
resource-depletion rates) and per-route data (travel duration, road condition, possible
hazard). Mamdani fuzzy inference systems turn these inputs into scores, and the scores
into objectives to be minimised: unfairness in prioritisation (ULPP), transport
infeasibility (TIL) and centre allocation imbalance (CAIL) in the three-objective
baseline, with TIL split into robustness and rapidity deficits in the four-objective
variant~\cite{rabiei2026resilient}. NSGA-II or NRGA search the allocations and return a
Pareto front; a committal rule picks one allocation from it. The reference
implementation is open source, continuously tested and dependency-audited, and records a
SHA-256 signature of every front together with an environment fingerprint.

We audit the three-objective baseline at the two reference sizes of the published study:
\emph{small} (5 centres, 150 people, 50 directed) and \emph{large} (10 centres, 300
people, 100 directed). Where a finding concerns the published experiments, we also check
the four-objective variant and the medium size.

\subsection{Why relief allocation is a turbulence case}
A disaster is an acute version of what an economic shock does slowly. Table~\ref{tab:mapping}
maps each input of the audited system to its economic counterpart, and each perturbation
mode of our turbulence model to the data failure it stands for. The mapping is at the level
of mechanism; we make no claim that numbers transfer.

\subsection{S1 --- Structural check}
Write each objective as a function of the decision variables and ask whether it
decomposes. In the audited model every objective is a mean, over the directed people, of a
score that depends only on the person (ULPP) or on the person and the assigned centre (TIL,
CAIL); centre occupancy enters as an input that the allocation does not change, and there
is no capacity constraint. For non-negative weights $w$, the weighted sum
\begin{equation}
\sum_k w_k f_k(d) \;=\; \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{dir}}}\sum_{j \text{ directed}}
\Bigl(w_1\,\mathrm{ULPP}_j + w_2\,\mathrm{TIL}_{j,c(j)} + w_3\,\mathrm{CAIL}_{j,c(j)}\Bigr)
\end{equation}
therefore separates by person: each person's best centre is independent of everyone
else's, and the optimum directs the $n_{\mathrm{dir}}$ people with the lowest best-case
cost. This greedy rule is exact, deterministic, and runs in $O(mn)$.

\subsection{S2 --- Exact reference and dominance}
We compute the exact equal-weight optimum and the exact \emph{supported} front by sweeping
the weight simplex on a regular lattice (21 steps per axis, 231 weight vectors). Each front
returned by the evolutionary system is compared with the exact optimum (how many of its
points the single exact point dominates) and with the exact supported front (hypervolume
share, with a common reference point at $1.1\times$ the component-wise maximum over all
fronts compared; unsupported Pareto points may exist and would only enlarge the exact
front).

\subsection{S3 --- Seed floor and budget curve}
The published system returns a front and leaves the final choice to the user; to audit a
committed decision we fix a rule, the front point minimising the equal-weight,
min--max-normalised objective sum. A rule that ignores the front's extent (nearest to the
ideal point in raw objectives) gives the same seed floor ($0.83$ and $0.94$ against $0.83$
and $0.94$), so the floor is not an artefact of the rule. We solve the clean instance under 20 seeds
($1000 + 7919k$, $k = 0,\dots,19$) and compare the committed decisions of every seed pair
(190 pairs): \emph{allocation churn} is the fraction of directed people whose assignment
differs; directed-set overlap is the Jaccard index of the two directed sets; quality is the
sum of the three objectives on clean data. Confidence intervals for pairwise statistics are
cluster-bootstrapped over seeds, because pairs share seeds. Repeating this at
$150$--$9600$ generations gives the budget curve: decision quality and identifiability
against compute.

\subsection{S4 --- Input turbulence on a noise-free decider}
Turbulence perturbs one raw input field at a time in one of four modes: additive Gaussian
noise with standard deviation a fraction of the field's range, systematic bias, missing
entries imputed with the instance median or mode, and flips of categorical status. Levels
are $0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4$; every perturbed value is clipped to its valid range; level
$0$ is an identity check. A decision is \emph{made} on degraded data and \emph{scored} on
the clean data. For a deterministic decider the difference between the two decisions'
clean scores is pure input sensitivity; for the exact decider it is the \emph{regret} of
deciding on degraded data, non-negative by construction. For the stochastic system the same
difference mixes input sensitivity with the seed floor of S3, so we report it above that
floor. Trends over levels use a one-sided Spearman test with realisations as units and Holm
correction across cells.

\subsection{S5 --- Repair and deployment envelope}
If S1 shows that the model cannot express a property its documentation claims, repair the
model minimally and re-run S2. Here the claim is that CAIL penalises concentrating load on
stressed centres; the repair makes occupancy endogenous,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{COR}'_i(\ell_i) = \min\Bigl(100,\; \mathrm{COR}_i + 100\,\frac{\ell_i}{\mathrm{cap}_i}\Bigr),
\qquad
\mathrm{CAIL}_{j,i} = \mathrm{FIS}_3\bigl(\mathrm{COR}'_i(\ell_i),\, \mathrm{RDR}_i,\, \mathrm{TD}_{j,i}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\ell_i$ is the number of people the decision sends to centre $i$. Capacity is soft
(faithful to the published semantics, with an overload diagnostic) or hard (a constraint).
The repaired model is no longer separable; we solve it exactly as a mixed-integer programme
in which only the load level of each centre is binary (Appendix~\ref{app:mip}), with HiGHS~\cite{huangfu2018highs}
through SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}. The envelope compares the time-limited MIP with the
evolutionary system given the same wall-clock time, on instances up to $3000$ people and
$50$ centres.

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}
All steps run on the open reference implementation (Python 3.12.13, NumPy 2.4.4, SciPy
1.17.1, DEAP 1.4.3, pymoo 0.6.2, scikit-fuzzy 0.5.0) on an Apple M4 under macOS 15.8; every
manifest records this fingerprint. Hypervolume is computed with pymoo's exact indicator. A
compiled evaluator reproduces the
scikit-fuzzy inference to $10^{-9}$ and is pinned to it by tests; the exact greedy and the
MIP are verified against brute-force enumeration on small instances. Every run writes a
manifest with seeds, budgets, instance hash and environment fingerprint; the data and code
are archived with a DOI (Data Availability Statement).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{RQ1 --- Necessity}\label{sec:rq1}
The structural check (S1) is decisive on its own: every objective of the published model
is a mean of per-person or per-(person, centre) scores, so the exact optimum of any
weighted sum is the greedy rule of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Excluding the fuzzy
pre-computation that both methods share ($0.05$--$0.22$\,s with the compiled evaluator,
$4$--$16$\,s with scikit-fuzzy), it runs in
about $0.01$--$0.02$\,ms on the reference instances, against seconds for one evolutionary
solve.
The equal-weight optimum has an objective sum of $74.58$ on the small instance and $85.18$
on the large one; the weight sweep yields an exact supported front of $181$ and $197$
points.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} compares this reference with the fronts the evolutionary system
returns. At the operational budget (150 generations in our runs, 200 in the published
experiments of~\cite{rabiei2026resilient}), the
single exact optimum dominates every point of every front: $2000$ of $2000$ points over
20 seeds at both sizes. The fronts cover $39\%$ (small) and $18\%$ (large) of the exact
supported front's hypervolume, and their best point is $17.8$ and $33.3$ objective units
worse than the exact optimum. Figure~\ref{fig:fronts} shows the gap in two projections.
More compute narrows it without closing it: at $9600$ generations, sixty-four times the
operational budget, the exact point still dominates $40\%$ (small) and $97\%$ (large) of
front points, and the hypervolume share reaches $0.80$ and $0.38$.

The gap is not specific to one solver, population size or instance. NRGA and NSGA-III
match NSGA-II at the operational budget; at $2400$ generations NRGA comes closest of the
three (best front point $77.30$ against $74.58$) but still covers only $64\%$ of the exact
hypervolume. Spending the same $240{,}000$ evaluations on a larger population (200 or 400)
instead of more generations changes the hypervolume share by at most $0.03$. On two further
generated instances per size the exact optimum dominates $80$--$100\%$ of front points.
The published experiments of~\cite{rabiei2026resilient} pass through the same check
(Table~\ref{tab:paperA}): across three sizes, both model variants, three algorithms and
thirty repetitions, one deterministic point dominates $97$--$100\%$ of all front points.

\subsection{RQ2 --- Identifiability}\label{sec:rq2}
Reproducibility holds, and across platforms: the same seed reproduces the same front bit
for bit within one environment, a decision recomputed from a stored front matched the
original run exactly, and a continuous-integration matrix (macOS arm64 and Ubuntu x86-64,
Python 3.11 and 3.12) produced identical front signatures for all ten (instance, seed)
combinations at the operational budget (Appendix~\ref{app:crossenv}). Identifiability does not.
Two runs on identical clean data with different seeds commit to allocations that differ for
$83.4\%$ [$82.0$, $84.7$] of the directed people on the small instance and $93.9\%$ [$93.3$,
$94.5$] on the large one (190 seed pairs each, cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI). The directed sets
overlap by a Jaccard index of only $0.31$ and $0.25$: most of the people one run chooses to
direct, the other run does not direct at all. The committed decisions also differ in quality,
with a standard deviation of $2.05$ (range $92.4$--$101.6$) and $1.32$ objective units.

Figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows how this floor responds to compute. On the small instance it
falls from $0.83$ to $0.57$ between $150$ and $9600$ generations while quality improves
from $97.4$ to $82.2$, still short of the exact $74.58$; on the large instance it stays
above $0.90$ throughout. The other solvers and instances give the same picture at the
operational budget (churn $0.83$ for NRGA and $0.84$ for NSGA-III; $0.79$--$0.95$ on the
alternate instances). The exact decider, by construction, has a floor of zero.

The floor also explains our original turbulence result. Comparing a decision made on
degraded inputs with the clean decision \emph{for the same seed}, the evolutionary system
re-routed about $83\%$ of the people at the smallest perturbation and stayed there. That
plateau equals the seed floor in every cell we measured (difference at most $0.02$). A changed
input changes the fitness landscape, and a changed landscape decorrelates the search as
completely as a new seed does. Missing centre-occupancy values show the mechanism exactly:
a realisation that blanks no centre leaves the inputs unchanged and the churn at zero, while
one that blanks any centre jumps to the floor, and the fraction of realisations with at
least one blanked centre predicts the mean churn to within $0.003$ at every level.

Small quality differences do not make these decisions interchangeable. In relief, as in any
rationing of scarce supply, the allocation is a list of named recipients: who is directed,
and where, is what the people affected experience and what an operator must justify. Two
allocations of near-equal aggregate quality that direct different people are different
decisions with different winners, and an audit trail that cannot say why one was chosen
over the other cannot carry accountability to the individuals concerned. Running the system
several times and committing to the consensus narrows the spread but does not close it
(Appendix~\ref{app:consensus}).

The answer to RQ2 is that the system is reproducible but not identifiable. Seed pinning
makes a run repeatable; it does not make its output \emph{the} decision, because the next
seed, equally valid, commits to a different allocation for most of the people.

\subsection{RQ3 --- Input fragility}\label{sec:rq3}
With solver noise removed, input fragility becomes measurable. Figure~\ref{fig:turbulence}
and Table~\ref{tab:rq3} report the regret of the exact decider, the clean-quality loss of
deciding on degraded data, over 50 realisations per level. Regret grows with the turbulence
level in all sixteen cells (eight field/mode cells at two sizes; one-sided Spearman,
$\rho = 0.37$--$0.97$, Holm-adjusted $p < 10^{-6}$). It is small at the lowest level ($\le
0.8$ objective units) and reaches $5$--$8$ units at level $0.4$ for the continuous inputs
the objectives consume (travel duration, resource time, damage level, centre occupancy),
with the largest cell mean, $14.5$, for centre-occupancy noise on the large instance (largest
single realisation $28.4$).
Categorical flips cost less ($2.7$--$3.8$), and missing values imputed by the median cost
least on the small instance; on the large one, missing centre-occupancy values cost $3.0$.
Allocation churn follows the same order: at most $10\%$ of the directed people change at
level $0.05$, and $3$--$70\%$ at level $0.4$.

\subsection{RQ4 --- Repair and deployment envelope}\label{sec:rq4}
The documentation of the audited system states that CAIL is low when load is spread and
rises when a decision piles people onto stressed centres~\cite{rabiei2026resilient}. The
published equations cannot express that: occupancy enters CAIL as a fixed input, so the
exact optimum sends $38$ of $50$ people to one centre on the small instance and $69$ of
$100$ on the large one. The repair of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} makes occupancy depend on
the assigned load, either softly (the objective sees the load; an overload diagnostic counts
people beyond free capacity) or with a hard capacity limit. We vary the capacity scale:
$\kappa$ sets centre size to $\lceil\kappa\, n_{\mathrm{dir}}/n\rceil$ in the soft variant
($\kappa = \infty$ recovers the published model), and $\kappa_f$ sets total free places to
$\kappa_f\, n_{\mathrm{dir}}$ in the hard one.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the audit changes for digital decision systems under turbulence}
Necessity comes first. A stochastic optimiser is a cost: compute, latency, and a decision
that changes from run to run. That cost buys something only when the problem is hard. In
the audited system it bought nothing, and no performance test would have shown it, because
every comparison in the original study~\cite{rabiei2026resilient} was between evolutionary
algorithms. That study named the gap itself and announced a benchmark against deterministic
weighted-sum and exact baselines as its next step; this audit delivers that step for its
published model, and the result shows why it should come first. Hooker's
warning~\cite{hooker1995testing} applies in its plainest form: the missing baseline was the
one that mattered. Checking whether the objectives decompose takes
minutes and should precede any choice of solver~\cite{kerschke2019selection,sorensen2015metaphor}.

Identifiability is a different property from reproducibility. The system logs seeds,
signatures and environment fingerprints, and every run can be repeated bit for bit. None of
that makes its output a decision. Two analysts who run the same tool on the same data with
different seeds will direct largely different people to different centres; a third run would
disagree with both. Under turbulence this is a governance problem before it is a technical
one: an allocation that cannot be told apart from an equally valid alternative cannot carry
the trust placed in it~\cite{jacovi2021trust}, and accountability frameworks that rely on
reproducing a decision~\cite{kroll2017accountable} are satisfied in letter only. For
seed-dependent systems, an audit should report the seed floor next to the result.

Fragility must be measured on a noise-free decider. Our first analysis of this system
reported that its decision collapses under the slightest input noise. That conclusion was an
artefact: the measured collapse was the seed floor, triggered by any change to the fitness
landscape. Only after removing solver noise did the actual input fragility appear, smooth,
monotone and concentrated in the inputs the objectives consume. This has a direct operational
consequence. Because the exact decider re-optimises in well under a second, including the rebuild of the
fuzzy scores that a data update invalidates, re-deciding on every update is a cheap guardrail, and data-quality effort can be targeted at the inputs with the
largest regret: here travel duration, remaining resource time and centre occupancy.

Expert rule bases have a dynamic range, and it bounds what an objective can express. The
CAIL rule base was written to penalise concentration, and once the model let it see the load
it pointed in the right direction, but with $17$--$20$ points of range over the full
occupancy scale it could not outweigh the other objectives. Capacity had to become a
constraint. Fuzzy objectives deserve the same structural scrutiny as the solver that
optimises them.

\subsection{Economic reading}
Each finding has a direct counterpart for an organisation that adopts an optimising AI system
to cope with economic turbulence (Table~\ref{tab:mapping}), and the protocol fits where such
adoption is decided: in procurement and governance, before the system is in use. Necessity: buying an AI optimiser
for a problem an exact rule solves is a cost without a quality return, and a structural check
before procurement is cheap. Identifiability: if two runs on the same market data allocate
scarce supply to different customers, the allocation policy is the random seed, not the
model; under uncertainty shocks~\cite{bloom2009uncertainty} that is the opposite of the
stability a decision system is bought to provide. Fragility: regret concentrated in a few
inputs tells the organisation which data feeds to harden first~\cite{wang1996dataquality,sambasivan2021cascades}.
Repair: if the model's stated semantics and its equations disagree, the equations decide what
the system does. These are mechanism-level claims; we do not transfer magnitudes from relief
to markets.

\subsection{Implications for our published work}
The equations and reported statistics of~\cite{rabiei2026resilient} are as stated, and its
fixed-budget comparisons between algorithms are unaffected in kind. Its central hypothesis,
that the robustness and rapidity deficits carry independent information, also holds on the
exact supported four-objective front: their Spearman correlation stays below the published
threshold of $|\rho| = 0.5$ at all three sizes ($-0.45$, $-0.21$, $-0.05$ with 455 weight
vectors; $-0.46$, $-0.21$, $-0.05$ with 1771), and the projection-dominance fraction remains
within its random-fusion null. The negative association that study read as a trade-off
between robust and fast routes, however, weakens with size and vanishes on the large exact
front, where the two deficits are close to independent.
Two points need correcting: the description of CAIL as penalising load concentration, which
its equations do not implement, and the reading of its fronts as trade-off surfaces for
practitioners, since the study did not compute the exact front and its fronts are far from
it (Table~\ref{tab:paperA}). The released tool also contains a separate humanitarian
allocation engine with a load-dependent overcrowding objective, an exact weighted-sum MILP and
a hard-capacity mode; the repair in Section~\ref{sec:rq4} brings the published model in line
with that design. The open artefact contains the exact decider and the repaired model, so
readers of the earlier study can reproduce both readings side by side.

\subsection{Limitations}
The audit covers one system, synthetic instances generated by its own instance generator,
and one application. The economic-turbulence setting is engaged through mechanisms, not
market data. Perturbations are applied to one field at a time; joint perturbations may
interact. The exact supported front omits unsupported Pareto points, so the reported
hypervolume shares are upper bounds on the evolutionary system's coverage. The MIP
formulation grows with people $\times$ centres $\times$ load levels; with soft capacity it
does not prove optimality beyond $1000\times25$ within five minutes, and at $3000\times50$ it
needs more than one minute to find a good incumbent. The time-matched comparison calibrates
generations once per instance and uses one solver configuration. The fuzzy rule bases are taken as published.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Before a digital AI decision system is trusted with turbulent conditions, ask whether its AI
is needed and whether its output is a decision. For the system we audited, our own, the
answers were no and no: a deterministic rule computes the exact optimum of its published
model in microseconds and dominates the evolutionary fronts, and the evolutionary system's
committed allocation changes for most people between two equally valid runs. With solver
noise removed, the system's true input fragility turned out to be smooth, measurable and
concentrated in a few inputs, which makes it manageable. A minimal repair of the model's
semantics is still solved exactly at the sizes the system targets. The five-step protocol
that produced these answers is generic, cheap to run, and checkable from the open artefact.
The next question is how often it would give the same answers for the optimising AI systems
now being deployed in supply chains under economic turbulence.

\section{Mixed-integer formulation of the repaired model}\label{app:mip}
Let $x_{j,i} \in [0,1]$ indicate that person $j$ is directed to centre $i$, let
$\mathcal{L}_i$ be the load levels of centre $i$, and let $y_{i,\ell} \in \{0,1\}$ indicate
that centre $i$ receives load level $\ell \in \mathcal{L}_i$; $z_{j,i,\ell} \in [0,1]$ links
the two. Levels at which $\mathrm{COR}'_i$ is clipped at $100$ share one CAIL value and are
merged into a single saturated level covering $[\ell^{\mathrm{sat}}_i, n_{\mathrm{dir}}]$.
For weights $w$,
\begin{align}
\min\; & \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{dir}}}\Bigl[\sum_{j,i} \bigl(w_1\mathrm{ULPP}_j + w_2\mathrm{TIL}_{j,i}\bigr)x_{j,i}
 + w_3 \sum_{j,i,\ell} \mathrm{CAIL}_{j,i}(\ell)\, z_{j,i,\ell}\Bigr] \\
\text{s.t.}\; & \textstyle\sum_i x_{j,i} \le 1 \;\; \forall j, \qquad \sum_{j,i} x_{j,i} = n_{\mathrm{dir}},
 \qquad \sum_{\ell} y_{i,\ell} = 1 \;\; \forall i,\\
& \textstyle\sum_{\ell} z_{j,i,\ell} = x_{j,i} \;\; \forall j,i, \qquad
 \underline{\ell}\, y_{i,\ell} \le \sum_j z_{j,i,\ell} \le \overline{\ell}\, y_{i,\ell} \;\; \forall i,\ell,
\end{align}
where $[\underline{\ell}, \overline{\ell}]$ is the load range of level $\ell$ (a single value
except for the saturated level). Hard capacity removes the levels above
$\lfloor \mathrm{cap}_i (1 - \mathrm{COR}_i/100)\rfloor$. Only $y$ is integer: for fixed $y$
the remaining problem is a transportation problem with integral data, whose linear relaxation
has integral vertices. We solve with HiGHS through \texttt{scipy.optimize.milp} under a
wall-clock limit and report the MIP gap. On instances small enough to enumerate, the MIP and
the separable greedy reach the brute-force optimum for three- and four-objective models, soft
and hard capacity, and $\kappa \in \{1, 3, \infty\}$ (test suite of the artefact).

\section{Consensus of several runs}\label{app:consensus}
A natural guardrail is to run the system several times and commit to the consensus. We
tested it offline on the stored decisions of the 20-seed floor: each person is directed by plurality vote over $k$ runs,
the $n_{\mathrm{dir}}$ people with the most votes are kept, and disjoint ensembles are
compared. Ten runs per ensemble lower the disagreement between ensembles from $0.83$ to $0.54$
(small) and from $0.94$ to $0.82$ (large), and improve quality to $82.1$ and $108.0$, at ten
times the compute and still short of the exact $74.58$ and $85.18$. Consensus also commits
to an allocation that no run produced, which weakens the audit trail it was meant to support. Disjoint ensembles of $k=3$ and $k=5$ runs fall in between
($0.73$ and $0.66$ small, $0.91$ and $0.88$ large).

On the large instance the MIP has several optimal allocations; HiGHS returns one on arm64 and
another on x86-64, each proven optimal with the same objective value. An audit trail for an
exact decider should therefore record the optimal value and a deterministic tie-breaking rule,
not only the allocation.
